How to choose a western bit, and fit it right

A plain English guide from the tack wall of our shop in Caldwell. What the different bits actually do, how to size one, and the adjustments that matter more than the bit itself.

Walk up to our tack wall and you will see dozens of bits hanging together, and to a newer rider they can all look about the same. They are not. A bit is a way of talking to your horse, and each style speaks a little differently. The right one makes your cues clear and quiet. The wrong one, or the right one adjusted badly, turns every ride into an argument. Here is how we walk folks through it at the counter.

Start with the horse, not the bit

The most common mistake we see is shopping for a bit to fix a problem. A horse that runs through the bridle, tosses its head, or will not stop is usually telling you something about training, teeth, or tack fit before it is telling you it needs more bit. Before you buy anything stronger, ask a few honest questions. When were his teeth last floated? Does he do this in every bit, or just this one? Is the headstall pinching or the curb strap too tight? More bit on a horse with a sore mouth only teaches him to brace harder. The best bit is usually the mildest one your horse listens to.

Snaffle or shank: the one difference that matters most

Here is the part that surprises people. A snaffle is not defined by a jointed mouthpiece. It is defined by how the rein connects. On a snaffle the rein attaches right at the mouthpiece ring, so when you pull an ounce, your horse feels an ounce. That direct, one to one feel is why snaffles are the go-to for young horses, for teaching, and for riding with two hands.

A shank bit, also called a curb or leverage bit, puts the reins on the end of a shank below the mouthpiece. That shank works like a lever, and paired with a curb strap under the chin, it multiplies whatever you do with your hands and adds pressure on the chin and poll. That is exactly what makes a shank bit so good for a finished horse ridden one handed, because a tiny lift of the rein is a clear signal. It is also why a shank bit in heavy or inexperienced hands can be a lot of bit. The longer the shank, the more leverage. Treat a shank bit as a refinement for a horse that already knows the job, not a set of brakes.

What about gag bits and barrel bits?

Speed events have their own family of bits. A gag bit lets the mouthpiece slide up the cheek when you pull, adding lift and poll pressure to help a horse rate and stay collected around a turn. Many barrel bits combine that with some leverage and a shaped mouthpiece. They can be exactly right for a seasoned barrel horse and completely wrong for a green one. Our oil slick barrel racing bit is a good example. The rainbow finish is what catches your eye, but whether it suits your horse depends on how far along he is. If you are not sure, call us and we will talk it through before you buy.

Reading the mouthpiece

Once you know the style, look at what sits in the mouth, because that is where most of the feel comes from.

  • Thicker is usually milder. A thicker mouthpiece spreads pressure over more of the tongue and bars. Very thick bits can crowd a horse with a small mouth or a fleshy tongue, though, so check that he can close his mouth comfortably.
  • A single joint gives a clear side to side signal but can fold and pinch in a nutcracker action when you take up both reins.
  • A three piece mouth, with a center link or roller, wraps more gently around the tongue and takes away most of that pinch.
  • A port is the upward curve in the middle of a solid mouthpiece. A low port gives the tongue a little room, which many horses appreciate. A tall port works on the roof of the mouth and belongs in experienced hands.
  • Sweet iron and copper encourage a horse to salivate and soften, which is why you see them on so many western bits. Stainless steel is easy to clean and does not rust.

Getting the size right

Most western bits come in a 5 inch mouthpiece, and that fits a large share of stock horses. It does not fit all of them. Ponies, Arabs, and fine boned horses often need a 4 3/4 or smaller, and big drafty types may need wider. You want about a quarter inch of mouthpiece showing on each side when the bit is centered. Too narrow and the cheeks pinch the corners of the mouth. Too wide and a jointed bit slides side to side and hangs low on the tongue. The easy way to check your horse is to lay a piece of baling twine across his mouth where the bit sits, mark each side at the corner of his lips, and measure the twine.

The adjustments that matter more than the bit

We would guess half the bit problems that come through our door are really adjustment problems. A great bit on a badly set headstall is still a bad ride.

  • Headstall height. For a snaffle, a good starting point is one soft wrinkle at the corners of the mouth. For a shank bit, the mouthpiece should rest comfortably against the corners without pulling them up into a smile. If the bit bangs on the teeth or the horse keeps playing with it, try a hole up or down.
  • Curb strap. On a shank bit, you should be able to slip two fingers flat between the strap and the chin. When you draw the reins back, the shanks should rotate to about a 45 degree angle before the strap engages. Too tight and the bit is always on. Too loose and the shanks swing back until they are nearly in line with the reins, and you lose the signal completely.
  • Headstall fit. The browband or ear loop should not pinch behind the ears, and the throatlatch, if there is one, should take a full hand held upright. A horse with a rubbed ear is not going to think much about your rein cues.

Buckles on both cheeks make all of this easier, because you can raise or lower the bit evenly instead of cranking one side. Our Wildcard tooled headstall and the Aztec print bridle both adjust on both sides, and they are the kind of tack people stop you about at the arena.

Signs the bit is not working

Your horse gives you feedback every ride. Gaping the mouth, tossing the head, getting his tongue over the bit, grinding his teeth, or leaning hard on your hands are all worth paying attention to. So are rubs or sores at the corners of the mouth or under the chin. None of these automatically mean you need a different bit. Check teeth and adjustment first, then think about whether the bit suits where your horse is in his training. If you change bits, change one thing at a time so you know what made the difference.

Show bits and good used bits

A show bit is usually a shank bit dressed up with engraved silver, built to look sharp under the lights and handled with care outside of the class. New silver show bits get expensive quickly, which is why our used tack is worth a look. We regularly have quality used bits at a fraction of the new price, like the silver mounted show bit with engraved cheeks we have in right now. Bits are some of the easiest used tack to buy with confidence, because a bit is solid metal. Check that the mouthpiece is smooth with no sharp edges, rough spots, or cracks at the joints, give it a good wash, and it is ready to ride.

The short version

  • Rule out teeth and tack fit before you reach for more bit.
  • Snaffles give direct feel. Shank bits add leverage, and suit a finished horse ridden one handed.
  • Thicker and three piece mouthpieces are generally milder.
  • Most stock horses take a 5 inch mouthpiece, but measure yours.
  • Set the headstall height and curb strap right. It matters as much as the bit.
  • Change one thing at a time and watch what your horse tells you.
